DeSci · The Thesis

Why most molecules die before the clinic

The failure rate isn't a bug. It's the reason early access is where the edge lives.

6 min readUpdated Nov 2026

Roughly nine in ten drug candidates never make it from the lab to the patient. Most die in the "valley of death" — the stretch between an exciting preclinical result and proof that it works in humans. If you're coming from investing, this number sounds terrifying. If you're coming from biotech, it's just Tuesday.

The instinct is to read the failure rate as a reason to stay out. That instinct is wrong — or at least, it's the wrong frame. The failure rate is exactly why the entry point matters more than the odds.

The valley of death

Between a molecule that works in a dish and a molecule that works in a person sits years of trials, hundreds of patients, and a mountain of capital. This is where good ideas go to die — not because the science was wrong, but because the funding and the patience ran out.

Traditional biotech handles this by concentrating risk: one company, one lead candidate, one binary outcome. You either own the company that clears the clinic, or you own the company that doesn't. There's very little in between.

You don't bet on one molecule. You hold the pipeline.

Early is where the edge lives

The reason early-stage is where the returns concentrate is simple: that's where the risk is priced highest, and where access is rarest. By the time a candidate is de-risked enough for the public markets, most of the move has happened.

DeSci changes the access, not the biology. Instead of waiting years to buy a single company's stock, you can hold a position across a pipeline — several programs at different stages — so the binary risk of any one molecule becomes a portfolio of them.

Liquidity changes the math

The old objection to early biotech wasn't the science — it was the lock-up. A decade, sometimes more, with no way out. DeSci's real contribution is making that position liquid: you can exit when your thesis changes, rather than waiting for an IPO that may never come.

None of this makes failure pleasant. It just makes it investable. You stop asking "will this one molecule work" — an unknowable — and start asking "am I positioned across the science that might." That's the question the pipeline answers.
